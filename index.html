Preamble

[cite_start]

The Notre Dame Law Review Editing Guide contains specific guidance and rules that are the final, highest authority for the Law Review. [cite: 35] [cite_start]To the extent that the Editing Guide conflicts with any other authority, the Editing Guide governs. [cite: 36]

[cite_start]

The Notre Dame Law Review prides itself on its accuracy, attention to detail, rigorous editing process, and punctuality. [cite: 37] [cite_start]This Editing Guide is designed to facilitate all of these goals and assist Staff Editors, the foundational component of the Law Review, in their pursuit of editing excellence. [cite: 38]

[cite_start]

The Notre Dame Law Review follows The Bluebook: A Uniform System of Citation (22nd ed.) and The Chicago Manual of Style (18th ed. 2024) for style, grammar, usage, and punctuation issues The Bluebook does not address. [cite: 39] [cite_start]The Notre Dame Law Review uses the Merriam-Webster.com Dictionary for spelling, definitional, and some usage issues. [cite: 40]

[cite_start]

The Editing Guide is divided into two books. [cite: 41] [cite_start]Book 1: Editing Procedures details the Law Review's processes for both source pull (acquiring and naming sources) and editing. [cite: 42] [cite_start]Rules in Book 1 may be cited by number with "EP" in front of them (e.g., EP 114 or EP 134.3). [cite: 43] [cite_start]Book 2: The Goldbook (this book) constitutes the Law Review's house style guide. [cite: 44] [cite_start]It builds on the rules provided by The Bluebook and The Chicago Manual of Style, providing clarity where these authorities are ambiguous, highlighting where Law Review practice differs from them, and communicating additional style instruction for editors. [cite: 45] [cite_start]Rules in Book 2 may be cited by number with "GB" in front of them (e.g., GB 1581). [cite: 46]

Chapter 1: Structure and Use of Citations

Section 100: Citation Basics

Rule 101. A citation footnote is always required to support the following:
  1. A direct quotation. (This rule does not apply to the use of scare quotes, to the use of quotation marks to denote a word as a word, or to the use of quotation marks to mark out a term of art whose source has already been properly footnoted in a previous appearance.) [cite_start][cite: 64]
    2. [cite_start]
  2. A source explicitly mentioned or referred to by implication. [cite: 65] For example: “In Marbury v. Madison, the Court recognized the power of judicial review.”
    3. [cite_start]
  3. A factual assertion that would not be obvious to a Staff Editor. [cite: 66] [cite_start]For example: “In 2003, the House considered 340 bills on infrastructure,” or “Approximately 32,000 fewer abortions occurred in the six months following Dobbs.” [cite: 67]
Rule 102. A citation footnote is not required to support the following:
    [cite_start]
  1. Introductory or summary paragraphs at the beginning or end of a series of substantive paragraphs, provided that the assertions made in the introductory or summary paragraphs are supported in the substantive paragraphs that follow, or precede, respectively. [cite: 68]
    2. [cite_start]
  2. Factual assertions that are obvious to a Staff Editor. [cite: 69] [cite_start]For example: “The United States is in North America,” “Alexander Hamilton wrote many of the Federalist papers,” or “A crime involves the union of mens rea and actus reus.” [cite: 70]
    3. [cite_start]
  3. Assertions that are not analysis or factual claims from other sources, but rather the author’s own analysis or argumentation. [cite: 71] [cite_start]For example: “It is worth emphasizing that the Supreme Court’s use of traditional rules of interpretation neither necessarily favored nor necessarily hindered federal power.” [cite: 72]
[cite_start]Rule 103. Staff Editors are never to add or remove a footnote in any round. [cite: 73] [cite_start]Footnote additions and deletions are solely for senior and executive edits. [cite: 74] [cite_start]A Staff Editor who thinks a footnote needs to be added must leave a comment explaining the need for a new footnote and providing a recommended citation for it, including acquiring and marking up a supporting source, as appropriate. [cite: 75] [cite_start]A Staff Editor who thinks a footnote needs to be deleted must leave a comment explaining why it should be removed. [cite: 76] (See Book 1: Editing Procedures, Rules 224 & 415.) [cite_start][cite: 77]
Rule 104. One Sentence Per Footnote. [cite_start]In general, the Law Review prefers that individual footnotes provide support for no more than a single sentence above the line. [cite: 79] [cite_start]As a result, if an author tries to use one footnote at the end of a sequence of sentences to provide support for all of them, editors should generally recommend the addition of footnotes at the end of each sentence. [cite: 80]
    [cite_start]
  • 104.1 Implementation of this rule may result in the creation of simple id. footnotes, but editors should still ensure that each footnote refers to only that portion of the source necessary to support the assertion in the specific sentence being footnoted. [cite: 81, 82]
    • [cite_start]
  • 104.2 This rule is especially true for sentences that contain direct quotations (each of which must have its own footnote), although it does not apply to quotations that are themselves more than one sentence long (in which case, a single footnote at the end of the quotation is the standard). [cite: 83]
    • [cite_start]
  • 104.3 This rule in no way prohibits the appearance of multiple footnotes supporting the same sentence, provided that the call numbers are placed in different positions with respect to the sentence. [cite: 84]
    • [cite_start]
  • 104.4 This rule may be violated for a good reason. [cite: 85]

Chapter 3: Subdivisions

Section 300: General Principles

[cite_start]Rule 301. Pincites Usually Required. Most citations require a pincite even though authors often fail to include them. [cite: 157] [cite_start]A pincite is required whenever it would help the reader find support in the source more quickly than if there were no pincite. [cite: 158] [cite_start]This means the support must appear in an identifiable portion of the source which can be indicated by a particular page, a page range, or another appropriate subdivision. [cite: 159]
[cite_start]Rule 302. Reasonable Effort. If an author did not include a pincite when a pincite is required under Rule 301 above, you must spend a reasonable amount of time trying to find the correct pincite. [cite: 160]
    [cite_start]
  • 302.1 If you do find it, you should (1) highlight the source file at that location and leave an appropriate source comment, (2) type a yellow-highlighted “PIN” in the proper pincite location in the footnote, and (3) recommend including the pincite you find in your comment on the footnote. [cite: 161] [cite_start]If the editor in the next round agrees with your suggestion, that editor should implement the pincite. [cite: 162]
    • [cite_start]
  • 302.2 If a reasonable amount of time and effort does not suffice to locate an appropriate pincite, you should leave a comment explaining the need for a pincite, as well as your effort and failure to find one. [cite: 163]
[cite_start]Rule 303. Omit a pincite only if the piece cites the source’s overall argument, cites the source without a signal because it is referred to in the text, cites the whole source as an example (see, e.g.,), uses the source to support something that the source clearly supports without saying so anywhere, or cites the source with a see generally signal. [cite: 164]

Chapter 5: Quotations

Section 500: Quotation Basics

[cite_start]Rule 501. Accuracy. Quotation marks must be placed so as to accurately reflect an author’s use of and reliance on the language of the author’s sources. [cite: 191] [cite_start]This may require moving quotation marks or adding quotation marks to include all words that match a source. [cite: 192]
[cite_start]Rule 502. Adding Quotation Marks. In general, any sequence of six or more words that exactly matches an author’s source must be put in quotation marks even if the author did not provide them. [cite: 193]
    [cite_start]
  • 502.1 This rule does not apply if there’s nothing particularly distinctive about the sequence of words (e.g., “the President of the United States”). [cite: 194]
    • [cite_start]
  • 502.2 This rule also does not apply if an ellipsis would be required to string together six or more words that match the source. [cite: 195]
    • [cite_start]
  • 502.3 This rule may apply even if one or more words need to be adjusted with brackets to make the match exact, depending on how distinctive the sequence of words is. [cite: 196]
    • [cite_start]
  • 502.4 Really distinctive phrases might need quotation marks even if they are shorter than six words. [cite: 197]
    • [cite_start]
  • 502.5 Bottom line: if you noticed that the author’s words sound like a quotation, it probably needs to be in quotation marks. [cite: 198]
[cite_start]Rule 503. Moving Quotation Marks. In general, if additional words on either side of an author’s quotation marks continue to match the source exactly, the quotation marks should be moved to include those words. [cite: 199]
    [cite_start]
  • 503.1 This rule does not apply to words which would require the addition of an ellipsis to be included in the quote. [cite: 200]
    • [cite_start]
  • 503.2 This rule also does not apply if the author leads in or out of a quotation with a small number of additional words which may fairly characterized as structural (e.g., “that,” “holding,” “in order to,” “is”). [cite: 201]
[cite_start]Rule 504. Extended Quotation. If an author joins two quotations from the same source with one or more words not in quotation marks which match the way the source joins the quoted words, delete the appropriate quotation marks to present this as one extended quotation. [cite: 202]
[cite_start]Rule 505. Short Phrases. Quoted phrases (like “to keep and bear arms”) which have already been properly cited do not need to be footnoted every time they appear in later discussion. [cite: 204]
[cite_start]Rule 506. Scare Quotes. Scare quotes must always be double quotation marks rather than single quotation marks (unless they appear within other double quotation marks). [cite: 205]
[cite_start]Rule 507. Run-In Quotations. To determine whether a run-in quotation should be preceded by a comma or begin with a capital letter, please consult CMOS 12.15 and 12.19, respectively, and other surrounding rules. [cite: 206]

Section 510: Formatting of Quotations (Bluebook rule 5.1)

[cite_start]Rule 511. Highlighting. Editors must highlight all quotations of fifty or more words in turquoise so that they can be formatted as block quotes. [cite: 208] [cite_start]This rule applies to quotes above the line and below the line. [cite: 209] [cite_start]This rule does not apply to quotations that appear inside parentheses. [cite: 210] [cite_start]Formatting for block quotes will be handled during senior and executive edits and should not be performed by Staff Editors. [cite: 211]
[cite_start]Rule 512. Spaces with Quotations. There should be no spaces between nested quotation marks, between a closing quotation mark and a comma, or between a closing quotation mark and a final punctuation mark. [cite: 212]

Section 520: Alterations and Quotations Within Quotations (Bluebook rule 5.2)

[cite_start]Rule 521. Periods and Commas. Brackets are never placed around a period or comma at the end of a quotation even if the punctuation does not match the source. [cite: 214]

Section 530: Omissions (Bluebook rule 5.3)

[cite_start]Rule 531. Ellipses. Omission of a word or words in a direct quote is indicated by the insertion of an ellipsis. [cite: 216] Do not use Word’s ellipsis character (…). [cite_start]Instead, ensure all ellipses are created by three periods each preceded by a nonbreaking space. [cite: 217]
    [cite_start]
  • 531.1 In accordance with Bluebook rules 2.1(g) and 2.2(d) from the 22nd edition, and Rule 212 above, ellipses and brackets always appear in roman type. [cite: 218]
[cite_start]Rule 532. Nonbreaking Spaces. A nonbreaking space differs from a normal space because nonbreaking spaces do not permit the characters immediately preceding and following them to break across lines. [cite: 219] [cite_start]This ensures that the periods used in ellipses will be kept together on the same line. [cite: 220] (Keyboard shortcuts for typing a nonbreaking space may be found in Book 1: Editing Procedures, Rule 247.) [cite_start][cite: 221]
[cite_start]Rule 533. Viewing Nonbreaking Spaces. In Word, nonbreaking spaces and normal (breaking) spaces can be observed by viewing nonprinting characters. [cite: 222] Click the pilcrow (¶) on Word’s Home tab. [cite_start]A normal (breaking) space will appear as an interpunct (·), while a nonbreaking space will appear as a degree sign (°). [cite: 223] [cite_start]A proper ellipsis followed by a period will appear as follows: °.°.°.°. [cite: 224]
[cite_start]Rule 534. Ellipsis with Additional Punctuation. When an ellipsis is followed by another punctuation mark (e.g., a comma or a period) without any intervening text, the space between the ellipsis and the following punctuation mark must also be nonbreaking (cf. CMOS 12.63). [cite: 225]
[cite_start]Rule 535. Space Following Ellipsis. When an ellipsis is followed by more quoted material that does not begin a new sentence in the source (even if language both at the end and after the end of a quoted sentence has been omitted), insert a normal (breaking) space after the ellipsis. [cite: 226]
[cite_start]Original: “Staff Editors are very busy. But they still enjoy Law Review.” [cite: 227] Incorrect: “Staff Editors°.°.°.°.·enjoy Law Review.” “Staff Editors°.°.°.°enjoy Law Review.” [cite_start]Correct: “Staff Editors°.°.°.·enjoy Law Review.” [cite: 228]
[cite_start]Rule 536. Quotation-Ending Ellipsis. When an ellipsis ends a quotation, ensure that one normal (breaking) space follows the closing quotation mark after the ellipsis’s final period and precedes the beginning of the next sentence, in accordance with Rule 231 above. [cite: 230]
[cite_start]Correct: "The cat bolted°.°.°.°."·It ran through the open door. [cite: 231]
[cite_start]Rule 537. Sentence-Ending Ellipsis Within a Quotation. When an ellipsis ends a sentence within a quotation and further quoted language follows an omission, give only one normal (breaking) space after the period ending the first quoted sentence (i.e., the fourth period in the ellipsis). [cite: 232] [cite_start]Each of the four periods should be preceded by a nonbreaking space in accordance with Rule 533 above. [cite: 233]
[cite_start]Correct: “A victim of crime may not seek his own retribution°.°.°.°.·[T]he state has a monopoly on violence.” [cite: 234]
[cite_start]Rule 538. Ellipsis After End of Sentence Within a Quotation. Where language after the end of a quoted sentence is deleted and is followed by further quotation (as in Bluebook rule 5.3(b)(v)), give only one nonbreaking space after the period ending the first quoted sentence, and one normal (breaking) space after the ellipsis. [cite: 236]
[cite_start]Correct: “National borders are less of a barrier to economic exchange now than at almost any other time in history.°.°.°.∙[E]conomic activity continues its relentless drive toward world-wide scope, [so] trademarks become even more important.” [cite: 237]

Chapter 6: Abbreviations, Numerals, and Symbols

Section 610: Abbreviations (Bluebook rule 6.1)

[cite_start]Rule 611. Introducing Abbreviations. Each first use of an uncommon abbreviation in the abstract, the main text, and the footnote text must be preceded by the full term. [cite: 239] [cite_start]The abbreviation must not have quotation marks around it. [cite: 240] [cite_start]The abbreviation usually follows immediately in parentheses, but it may be introduced in other ways per the examples in CMOS 10.3. [cite: 241]
Incorrect: The DOS has temporarily closed. [cite_start]The Department of State (“DOS”) has temporarily closed. [cite: 242] [cite_start]Correct: The Department of State (DOS) has temporarily closed. [cite: 243]
[cite_start]Rule 612. Common Abbreviations. Common abbreviations are those that have their own separate entry (for the abbreviation) in the Merriam-Webster.com Dictionary. [cite: 244] [cite_start]If a Merriam Webster abbreviation has more than one definition, it is not a common abbreviation and must be spelled out per Rule 611 above. [cite: 245] [cite_start]Search the Merriam-Webster.com Dictionary for the abbreviation to confirm. [cite: 246] [cite_start]Some examples of common abbreviations with a single definition in the Merriam-Webster.com Dictionary are DOD, FBI, NCAA, ACLU, NATO, and DOJ. [cite: 247]
[cite_start]Rule 613. Abbreviating Hyphenated Words. When an abbreviation is permitted, abbreviate hyphenated words of which one or more of the component words has a designated abbreviation in the appropriate abbreviation table in The Bluebook. [cite: 248] [cite_start]Do not, however, abbreviate a closed compound unless the entire word has a designated abbreviation in the appropriate abbreviation table. [cite: 249]
Incorrect: 47 Chicago-Kent L. Rev. 1234 Jones v. Mid-America Bank Correct: 47 Chi.-Kent L. Rev. 1234 Jones v. Mid-Am. Bank [cite_start]Jones v. MidAmerica Bank [cite: 250, 251]
[cite_start]Rule 614. Commas After Certain Abbreviations. The abbreviations “e.g.” and “i.e.” [cite: 251] [cite_start]must always be immediately followed by a comma, even when used by the author in ordinary text above the line. [cite: 252]
[cite_start]Rule 615. No Italics in Ordinary Usage. Although certain abbreviations (like “e.g.” and “cf.”) must be italicized when used as signals in accordance with Bluebook rule 2.1(d), the Law Review otherwise follows CMOS 10.7 in only italicizing those abbreviations which stand for terms which would need to be italicized if spelled out. [cite: 254] [cite_start]This means that in ordinary usage above the line, these abbreviations (and others like “i.e.” or “et al.”) should generally appear in ordinary roman typeface. [cite: 255]
[cite_start]Rule 616. Versus. In ordinary text (as opposed to a citation or a heading), the word “versus” should always be spelled out. [cite: 256] [cite_start]Neither “v.” nor “vs.” should appear in italics unless it is part of the name of a case being referred to or otherwise part of a name or title. [cite: 257]
[cite_start]Rule 617. Periods with Business Designations. When referring to companies, whether directly or in a case name, if an abbreviated term like “L.L.C.” [cite: 258] [cite_start]or “N.A.” cannot be omitted under Bluebook rule 10.2.1(h), it should be spelled with periods or without in accordance with the company’s own practice, as reflected in the case caption. [cite: 259]
[cite_start]Rule 618. Periods in Abbreviations. The general principle followed by The Bluebook is that abbreviations should end with a period unless there is an apostrophe setting off either the first or last letter of the abbreviation (which is usually the choice when the last letter of the full word is the last letter of the abbreviation). [cite: 260] [cite_start]This rule does not supersede anything specified by The Bluebook: it is only intended to help editors make sense of The Bluebook. [cite: 261]
[cite_start]Rule 619. The United States. The term “United States” should be abbreviated as “U.S.” [cite: 262] when and only when it is being used as an adjective. [cite_start]“United States” and “U.S.” [cite: 263] [cite_start]are to be preferred to “America” or “American” unless common usage favors the latter. [cite: 264]

Section 620: Numerals and Symbols (Bluebook rule 6.2)

[cite_start]Rule 621. Numbered or Lettered Items. In running text numbered or lettered items should be indicated with the number or letter enclosed in parentheses on both sides like this: (2) or (a). [cite: 266] [cite_start]A nonbreaking space is required between the closing parenthesis and the text following it. [cite: 267] (This rule does not apply to numbered or lettered lists presented on separate lines in the manner of bullet points.)
[cite_start]Rule 622. Multiples of round numbers, as described in Bluebook rule 6.2(a)(ii), may be written with the multiple as a numeral and the largest whole number spelled out. [cite: 268] [cite_start]For example: 2 billion, 500 thousand. [cite: 269]
[cite_start]Rule 623. Decades. Decades are properly spelled without an apostrophe (e.g., 1930s), in accordance with CMOS 9.35. [cite: 270]
[cite_start]Rule 624. Nonbreaking Spaces with Symbols. All section and paragraph symbols must be separated from their corresponding numeral by a nonbreaking space. [cite: 271] (Keyboard shortcuts for typing a section symbol or a paragraph mark (pilcrow) [cite_start]may be found in Book 1: Editing Procedures, Rule 247.) [cite: 272]
Incorrect: §·1987 ¶·1987 Correct: §°1987 ¶°1987
[cite_start]Rule 625. Paragraph Symbols. Paragraph symbols should only appear in citations for appropriate sources, never in ordinary text, whether above or below the line. [cite: 273]
[cite_start]Rule 626. Ampersands. Ampersands (&) should only be used in accordance with Bluebook rules that require them (such as 15.1(a)) or where they appear in a title or name (like that of a law firm). [cite: 274] [cite_start]An ampersand should never appear in ordinary text where the word “and” would work. [cite: 275]
[cite_start]Rule 627. Currency Symbols. Always use appropriate currency symbols ($, £, €, etc.) to refer to specific quantities of money, and never put a space between the symbol and the numeral. [cite: 276]

Section 1000: General Principles

[cite_start]Rule 1001. Syllabus. In general, you cannot cite to the syllabus of a case; [cite: 311] [cite_start]pincites should always be to the text of the opinion. [cite: 312]
    [cite_start]
  • 1001.1 If an author gives a pincite to the syllabus, you should find the corresponding passage in the opinion itself and suggest replacing the pincite. [cite: 313]

  • 1001.2 This rule always applies to opinions of the Supreme Court of the United States. [cite: 314]
    • [cite_start]
  • 1001.3 Exceptions to this rule may be allowed in cases where the syllabus is written by the court itself, but only if it is perfectly clear that the court is the author of the syllabus. [cite: 315] Some state supreme courts write their own syllabuses.
